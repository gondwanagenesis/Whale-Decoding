What We Know So Far

Sperm whales communicate using codas: short, patterned sequences of clicks. For decades, researchers knew whales produced distinct click patterns, but lacked the tools to find structure in them. That changed in 2024 when machine learning revealed that codas aren't just different patterns—they form a combinatorial system, with four independently modulated properties (rhythm, tempo, rubato, and ornamentation) that combine to produce a large repertoire.

The 2026 follow-up discovered that individual clicks within codas carry vowel-like formant structures. Two types—"a-codas" (one formant) and "i-codas" (two formants)—pattern the way vowels do in human speech, including duration contrasts between short and long forms.

What this doesn't mean. No one has decoded what any specific coda means. The work shows structure—an alphabet, not a dictionary. The researchers themselves caution that meanings, social functions, and whether this qualifies as "language" in the full sense remain unknown.

The Major Players

Project CETI (Cetacean Translation Initiative)

The flagship effort. Founded by marine biologist David Gruber, ML researcher Michael Bronstein, and biologist Shane Gero (whose Dominica Sperm Whale Project provides the core dataset of ~100,000 entries). CETI now has 50+ researchers across MIT, UC Berkeley, Northwestern, and other institutions. Funded by the Audacious Project (TED). Their approach: record everything, find structure with ML, then work toward meaning.

Earth Species Project (ESP)

A nonprofit focused on bioacoustics AI across many species. Released NatureLM-audio, a foundation model for animal sounds across thousands of species. Their killer whale work with Raincoast Conservation Foundation is expanding in 2026. Co-organized the first AI for Non-Human Animal Communication workshop at NeurIPS. Less focused on translation, more on detection, classification, and monitoring tools.

Dominica Sperm Whale Project

The long-running field operation (Shane Gero) that produces the recordings CETI's ML work depends on. Over two decades of data from one Eastern Caribbean clan of females and young. This is where the 8,719 codas in the 2024 Nature Communications paper came from, and the 3,948 codas in the 2026 Proc. R. Soc. B vowel paper.

Synthesis: What dialogue patterns tell us. Sperm whale conversation is structurally unlike human dialogue. It is bimodal (echo or contrast, rarely switching), immediate (tempo match happens on the first exchange or not at all), terse (one coda per turn), and flat (no warm-up, no crescendo, no closing ritual). The 5-click coda dominates dialogue the way common words dominate human speech—it is the medium of echo-based matching, the shared vocabulary that both whales can produce and mirror. When whales do break the echo, they modulate tempo, not click count. This two-channel structure (click count for "what type" and tempo for "what variant") functioning differently in dialogue than in repertoire statistics is evidence of pragmatic structure—rules governing how codas are used in context, distinct from the combinatorial rules governing how they are constructed.

Information Theory of Whale Dialogue

We applied information-theoretic measures to the full dialogue corpus to quantify how much structure whale coda sequences carry, and how it compares to known communication systems.

Entropy Rate: How Predictable Are Whale Sequences?

The entropy rate measures how many bits of surprise each new coda carries, given the preceding context. Lower is more predictable.

3.22
No context (bits)
2.10

1.36

Key finding: With three codas of context, each new coda carries only 0.95 bits of information—a 70% reduction from the no-context baseline. For comparison, English text has an entropy rate of ~1.0–1.3 bits per character (Shannon, 1951). Whale dialogue, measured per coda rather than per character, has comparable sequential structure. A random sequence over the same 78-class vocabulary would carry 6.29 bits—whale sequences are 85% below random.

Zipf's Law

1.72

Coda class frequencies follow Zipf's law with exponent 1.72 (R² = 0.965, p < 10&sup{-56}). For comparison, human language word frequencies follow Zipf's law with exponent ≈1.0. The steeper whale exponent means coda usage is more concentrated than human word usage—the top few types dominate more heavily. This is characteristic of communication systems with a "common ground" vocabulary (the 5-click codas) that serves social bonding, alongside a more diverse repertoire for less frequent functions.

We shuffled coda sequences within exchanges (preserving exchange boundaries and marginal frequencies) 200 times. The real trigram entropy (1.36 bits) is 28 standard deviations below the shuffled mean (1.77 bits). Whale dialogue sequences contain structure that cannot be explained by local transition probabilities alone. This is necessary (but not sufficient) evidence for grammar-like organization.

Repetition Structure

67% of all codas fall within exact-repetition runs (same class repeated 2+ times in a row). The mean run is 5.4 codas, with the longest at 59. This is the acoustic substrate of the "echo mode" observed in dialogue: long chains of repeated coda classes, contributed by both whales alternately.

Exchange Opening Predictability

When an exchange opens with two 5-vslow codas, the third coda is 5-vslow 98% of the time (56/57 exchanges). 5-fast openings predict 5-fast continuation 95% of the time. The first two codas of an exchange almost completely determine its trajectory—this is the bimodal echo/contrast structure emerging from the information-theoretic perspective.

Four functional categories emerge:

4. Closing signals (7-slow, 7-fast): 4–5x overrepresented as the final coda in exchanges.

Seasonal Patterns

Recording spans January–June (the breeding season off Dominica). Repertoire diversity peaks in February–April (3.0–3.3 bits entropy across 25–35 types) and drops in May–June (1.7–1.8 bits, 19–21 types). This seasonal shift in communicative diversity may reflect changing social contexts as breeding season progresses.

Syntax Probes

We tested for hierarchical structure using three approaches. None is definitive, but together they constrain what kind of grammar whale sequences could have.

Center-Embedding (A-X-A Patterns)

A-X-A patterns (where the same class brackets a different one) occur 21% above chance (17.5% observed vs 14.5% expected). The top pattern is 5-vslow → 5-slow → 5-vslow (61 occurrences)—a tempo-shift sandwich. This is consistent with, but doesn't prove, hierarchical nesting.

Controlled Long-Range Information

0.75
CMI (bits beyond adjacent)

The coda two steps back carries 0.75 bits of information about the current coda beyond what the immediate predecessor reveals. This is large—it means that knowing what came before the last coda makes the next coda substantially more predictable. Formally: CMI(Xn; Xn−2 | Xn−1) = 0.747 bits. This is strong evidence for supra-Markov structure—not proof of hierarchy, but proof that whale sequences have memory beyond simple pair transitions.

We can't know what codas mean, but we can bound how much meaning an exchange could carry.

An average exchange (20 codas) carries about 19 bits of non-redundant information (at 0.95 bits/coda after contextual compression). That's roughly equivalent to 4 English words. 70% of the raw coda stream is predictable from context—it's echo and repetition, not new information.

What this means: Whale exchanges are overwhelmingly about social coordination—mutual recognition, bonding, and maintaining a conversational state—rather than dense content transmission. The 19-bit payload is consistent with exchanges that convey identity, social relationship, and current state ("I'm here, I'm with you, we're in this mode") rather than referential content ("there's food at that location"). This doesn't preclude semantics, but it constrains the kind of semantics: phatic communion, not information exchange. In human terms, whale dialogue is closer to a long handshake than to a sentence.

Individual Identity

The Dominica dataset includes 36 individually identified whales across 13 social units.

Individual Repertoire Signatures

Individual whales have dramatically different repertoire profiles. Whale 5978 uses coda type 1+1+3 for 96% of all codas (extremely conservative), while whale 5722 uses the rare type 4D for 56% (highly distinctive). Individual variation is large enough that same-unit whales are not statistically more similar than different-unit whales (JSD = 0.516 vs 0.529, p = 0.72).

Unit "dialects" are not about repertoire proportions. Social units share the same coda types but vary widely in how individual members use them. This suggests that unit-level dialect markers operate at subtler levels—timing, spectral features, or context-dependent usage—not at the level of which types are used most. Unit J has the most internally consistent members (mean JSD 0.37), while Unit F is the most diverse (0.66).

Language Properties Scorecard

How does sperm whale communication measure against the standard linguistic criteria for language? We evaluate each property using our analysis of the Dominica dataset combined with published research.

The framing matters. "Is this language?" is the wrong question. Language isn't binary—it's a cluster of properties (Hockett, 1960). Whale communication clearly has some of these properties and clearly lacks evidence for others. The scorecard below tries to be precise about what we know and what we don't.
Confirmed

Combinatoriality (Duality of Patterning)

Codas are built from four independently modulated features (rhythm, tempo, rubato, ornamentation), producing 156+ distinguishable types. This is the whale equivalent of phonemes combining into words. Source: Sharma et al. 2024

Confirmed

Phonology (Sound Structure)

Individual clicks carry vowel-like formant structures: "a-codas" (one formant) and "i-codas" (two formants), with short/long duration contrasts. This is structural organization at the sub-coda level. Source: Beguš et al. 2026

Confirmed

Zipf's Law Distribution

Coda frequencies follow Zipf's law with exponent 1.72 (R² = 0.965). This power-law distribution is a hallmark of natural language and is not found in random or purely mechanical signals. The steeper-than-English exponent suggests high concentration on common social codas.

Confirmed

Sequential Structure (Beyond-Markov)

Coda sequences carry structure that cannot be explained by simple transition probabilities (z = −28 vs shuffled controls). The entropy rate drops 70% with 3-coda context, and mutual information persists at 13% even at 20 codas distance. This is not random vocalization.

Confirmed

Turn-Taking

Median turn gap of 0.52s with 40.6% overlap rate—tighter temporal coordination than human conversation (~200ms gap, ~17% overlap). 74% of turns are single-coda, showing one-unit-per-turn structure analogous to adjacency pairs in human dialogue.

Confirmed

Dialogue Coordination / Symmetry

100% of two-whale exchanges show symmetric initiative (no leader/follower). Echo behavior is mutual (r = 0.95 between echo given and echo received). Mutual information between prompt and response is 0.87 bits—whales respond to each other, not independently.

Confirmed

Pragmatic Structure (Context-Dependent Use)

5-click codas function differently in dialogue (57% echo rate) than in the overall repertoire. Echo/contrast bimodality (P=0.82 persistence) shows that codas are used according to conversational rules distinct from their acoustic properties. Tempo serves as a contrast channel independently of click count. This is evidence of pragmatics—rules governing use in context.

Confirmed

Exchange Typology

Exchanges cluster into three types: echo-dominant (48%, mean echo rate 90%), contrast-dominant (34%, echo rate 3%), and mixed (18%). 70% of network edges are reciprocal, and 67% of all codas are in repetition runs. The exchange type is determined in the first two codas and persists throughout.

Plausible

Productivity (Novel Combinations)

78 coda classes are actively used in dialogue, and the combinatorial system (4 features × multiple levels) can generate 156+ types. The beyond-Markov structure suggests rule-governed sequencing rather than memorized chunks. But we cannot distinguish truly novel sequences from a large memorized repertoire without semantic grounding.

Unknown

Syntax (Hierarchical Structure)

Beyond-Markov structure is strong: z = −28 vs shuffled controls, and 0.75 bits of conditional mutual information beyond the adjacent coda. A-X-A center-embedding patterns occur 21% above chance. But we still cannot distinguish finite-state grammar (regular, like a playlist on shuffle within modes) from context-free grammar (hierarchical, like nested clauses). The structure we see is consistent with either. Resolving this requires controlled experiments or much larger corpora with richer feature sets.

Unknown

Semantics (Meaning)

No coda has been mapped to a meaning. We bounded the semantic payload at ~19 bits per exchange (~4 English words), with 70% of the signal being echo/redundancy. The functional roles we identified (opener, echo, contrast, closer) are positional statistics, not meanings. The low content density suggests phatic/social communication rather than referential content, but playback experiments with behavioral observation are needed to test this.

Unknown

Displacement (Reference to Absent Things)

Cannot be tested without semantic grounding. If whales can "talk about" absent pod members, locations, or past events, displacement exists—but no observation or experiment has addressed this.

Score: 8 confirmed, 1 plausible, 3 unknown. Sperm whale communication satisfies more structural criteria for language than any non-human system previously studied. The three unknowns (semantics, syntax, displacement) are the hardest to test and the most definitional—but our analysis has narrowed each. Syntax has strong beyond-Markov structure (0.75 bits CMI) but we can't yet prove hierarchy. Semantics is bounded at ~19 bits/exchange, consistent with social/phatic function. What we can say: this system has the infrastructure for language—combinatorial coding, phonological structure, Zipf-distributed usage, strong sequential dependencies, tight turn-taking, pragmatic rules, cooperative symmetry, and individual variation. Whether that infrastructure carries meaning in the linguistic sense remains the central open question—but the specific experiments to answer it are now designable from the structural patterns we've identified.

Formal Grammar of Whale Dialogue

Distilled from 3,839 dialogue codas across 89 recorded exchanges. These rules form the minimal description that captures the observed patterns.

Rule 1: Two-State Conversational Machine

Every whale-switch in dialogue is either an Echo (same coda class repeated) or a Contrast (different class). These two modes behave like states in a Markov chain with strong self-persistence:

TransitionProbabilityMean run
Echo → Echo82.1%5.6 turns before switching
Contrast → Contrast85.7%7.0 turns before switching

Stationary distribution: 44.5% Echo, 55.5% Contrast. The system spends slightly more time in contrast mode, where whales trade different coda types back and forth.

Rule 2: Class Selection Within Mode

Echo mode (n=1,007): Almost entirely 5-click codas. The dominant echo class is 5-vslow at 65.3%, followed by 5-slow (17.9%) and 5-fast (13.1%). Top 5 classes cover 99.3% of all echoes.

Contrast mode (n=1,221): How whales differ when responding:

Contrast mechanismFrequency
Both tempo + click count change47.1%
Tempo shift only33.9%
Click count shift only19.0%

When contrasting, whales most often change both dimensions simultaneously—a full coda-class switch rather than a minimal edit.

Rule 3: Turn Structure

Turn-taking follows a geometric distribution with P(yield) = 0.74 per coda. This means:

Turn lengthProbability
1 coda (immediate yield)73.8%
2 codas14.2%
3 codas5.1%
4+ codas6.9%

Mean turn: 1.57 codas. Maximum observed: 41 consecutive codas from one whale (a monologue event). The system is overwhelmingly single-coda turns—strict call-and-response.

Rule 4: Opening Protocol

The first coda in an exchange predicts the initial conversational mode:

OpenerPredicted modeConfidencen
5-vslowEcho90%42
5-fastEcho77%13
8-vfastContrast83%6
5-mediumContrast75%16
5-slowContrast75%12

Slow 5-click codas invite echoing (social bonding); fast or non-5-click codas invite contrast (information exchange).

Complete Grammar (pseudocode)
Exchange :=
  opener_coda   → determines initial_mode
  repeat {
    current_mode → selects coda_class
    yield? (P=0.74/coda) → switch speaker
    mode_transition:
      if Echo:     stay Echo (P=0.82)
      if Contrast: stay Contrast (P=0.86)
  } until exchange ends

Modal rules:
  Echo:     response = prompt (same class)
            dominant: 5-vslow (65%), 5-slow (18%)
  Contrast: response ≠ prompt
            usually both tempo + clicks change (47%)

Opening rules:
  5-vslow opener → Echo mode  (90%)
  5-fast  opener → Echo mode  (77%)
  8-vfast opener → Contrast   (83%)
  5-medium       → Contrast   (75%)

Grammar completeness: State persistence alone predicts 84.1% of transitions correctly. Residual entropy: 1.36 bits/symbol (down from 3.22 at order 0—a 58% reduction from grammar alone).

Cross-Species Communication Comparison

How do sperm whale codas compare to other animal communication systems? We evaluate seven systems on the same axes: vocabulary, entropy, syntax, Hockett's design features, and semantic capacity.

Where Sperm Whales Stand Out

Highest Hockett score among non-human animals (8/12). The only animal system with confirmed: combinatorial coding (4 independent features), productive syntax (supra-Markov), dialogic turn-taking, and cultural transmission.

Only non-human system with confirmed supra-Markov grammar. While humpback songs have hierarchical structure, that structure is in one-way broadcasts. Sperm whale codas show grammar in dialogue—the conversational partner's choice depends on context beyond the immediately preceding coda (CMI = 0.75 bits at distance 2).

The highest vocabulary of any dialogic animal system. 156 distinguishable coda types exceeds any other measured call-and-response system. Dolphins have more whistles, but they function primarily as identity signals, not combinatorial elements in structured exchanges.

Where Sperm Whales Fall Short

No confirmed semantics, displacement, or meta-language. Our 19-bit semantic bound is consistent with phatic (social bonding) communication rather than propositional content. Bee dances actually encode more semantic information (distance + direction to food) in a simpler system.

Entropy rate near human language, but for different reasons. Sperm whale entropy (0.95 bits/coda) and English text (~1.0 bits/character) are remarkably similar, but human language achieves this through recursive grammar over an enormous vocabulary. Whale codas achieve it through strong mode persistence and a concentrated distribution—70% of communication is redundant social signal.

No recursion detected. Our center-embedding test found no evidence of nested structures. The grammar is supra-Markov but appears to be a finite-state (regular) grammar, not context-free. This is the ceiling of what ICI-based data can test.

Sperm whales have the steepest Zipf exponent of any dialogic system. This is consistent with social communication: a small set of codas carries most of the interaction (identity + bonding), while the long tail of rare types may serve specialized functions.

The Big Picture

Sperm whale communication sits in a unique position: the most complex dialogic system in the animal kingdom, but still fundamentally social rather than propositional.

It is not a “language” in the human sense—there is no evidence of displacement, recursion, or open-ended semantics. But it is far more structured than any other animal call-and-response system. The combination of combinatorial coding, supra-Markov grammar, functional differentiation, and bimodal dialogue structure has no known parallel outside humans.

The question is no longer “do whales communicate?” but “what is the 19 bits per exchange actually encoding?” Identity, social status, emotional state, group coordination—or something we haven't considered?

Open Questions & Future Directions

What do any codas mean? fundamental

Structure has been found (alphabet, vowels), but no coda has been mapped to a meaning. The researchers note that understanding semantic capabilities will require in-depth and interactive research, including playback experiments in natural settings.

Is this language? definitional

Combinatorial structure and phonology are necessary conditions for language but not sufficient. Human language also requires syntax, semantics, displacement (talking about absent things), and productivity (novel sentences). None of these have been demonstrated in whale codas yet.

How do other populations differ? generalization

Almost all ML work uses recordings from one clan off Dominica. Other sperm whale populations likely communicate in different ways. The Mediterranean dialect report (if confirmed) would be the first step toward comparative phonology.

Can WhAM's representations reveal semantics? ML frontier

WhAM was trained for generation but developed useful classification representations as a byproduct. If paired with behavioral data (what were the whales doing when they produced each coda?), these representations might help cluster codas by function.

Data scale infrastructure

Estimates of data needed vary wildly: some experts cite a billion clicks (~100–200 million codas), while WhAM was trained on 10k recordings. The AVATARS robotic framework should accelerate collection, but the gap between current data and what translation might require remains large.

Ethical boundaries ethics

Should researchers play back synthetic codas to wild whales? Playback experiments are seen as a necessary next step for validating meaning, but could also disturb the animals. Commentators have also flagged concerns about tech company funding and potential exploitation of findings.

What Our Analysis Narrows Down

Based on our information-theoretic and dialogue analysis, we can now be more specific about what's known and what's not.
The semantic grounding problem is now specific narrowed

We've identified four functional coda categories (echo, opener, contrast, closer) from positional statistics alone. Behavioral annotation doesn't need to start from scratch—it can target these categories specifically. If 4-vslow really is a "topic shift" and 8-vfast is an "attention getter," playback experiments can test those hypotheses directly.

The syntax question has a testable form narrowed

We've shown beyond-Markov structure (z = −28) but can't distinguish finite-state from context-free grammar. The specific test: do whale exchanges contain center-embedding (A-B-A where A and B can themselves be structured)? Our data shows A-B-A patterns, but at the coda-class level, not at a structural level. Larger corpora with spectral features (vowel data) could resolve this.

The channel capacity is a lower bound narrowed

Our 0.87-bit MI between prompt and response classifies codas only by click-count + tempo-band, discarding spectral (vowel) information. The 2026 vowel paper shows that spectral features carry independent information. True channel capacity is higher, and integrating vowel features into dialogue analysis is the most direct next step for this dataset.

Build-On-Others Opportunities

Behavioral annotation (targeted)

Pair our four functional categories with behavioral observation. Specifically: do "opener codas" (8-vfast, 9-vfast) correlate with approach behavior? Do "contrast codas" (4-vslow) correlate with direction changes or disengagement? These are testable predictions from the dialogue analysis.

Vowel-dialogue integration

The 2026 Beguš paper and our dialogue analysis use overlapping but not identical datasets. Integrating formant features (a-coda vs i-coda) with our dialogue role analysis could reveal whether vowel-like features carry meaning in conversational context—e.g., do a-codas and i-codas serve different pragmatic functions?

Cross-population dialogue comparison

Our dialogue structure (bimodal echo/contrast, 5-click dominance, functional differentiation) is measured on one Caribbean clan. Testing these patterns in Pacific, Mediterranean, or other Atlantic populations would reveal whether dialogue structure is species-universal or culturally learned.

WhAM embedding + dialogue position

WhAM's learned representations encode acoustic information our ICI-based features miss. Probing WhAM embeddings with our dialogue-position labels (opener, echo, contrast, closer) could reveal whether the acoustic features that distinguish these roles are captured by the generative model's internal representations.

Information-theoretic playback design

Our entropy rate (0.95 bits with context) and transition preferences tell us exactly how surprising each possible response is. A playback experiment could present whales with sequences of varying surprise—from maximally predictable (echo chains) to maximally surprising (high-entropy contrasts)—and measure behavioral response as a function of information content.
