What We Know So Far

Sperm whales communicate using codas: short, patterned sequences of clicks. For decades, researchers knew whales produced distinct click patterns, but lacked the tools to find structure in them. That changed in 2024 when machine learning revealed that codas aren't just different patterns—they form a combinatorial system, with four independently modulated properties (rhythm, tempo, rubato, and ornamentation) that combine to produce a large repertoire.

The 2026 follow-up discovered that individual clicks within codas carry vowel-like formant structures. Two types—"a-codas" (one formant) and "i-codas" (two formants)—pattern the way vowels do in human speech, including duration contrasts between short and long forms.

What this doesn't mean. No one has decoded what any specific coda means. The work shows structure—an alphabet, not a dictionary. The researchers themselves caution that meanings, social functions, and whether this qualifies as "language" in the full sense remain unknown.

The Major Players

Project CETI (Cetacean Translation Initiative)

The flagship effort. Founded by marine biologist David Gruber, ML researcher Michael Bronstein, and biologist Shane Gero (whose Dominica Sperm Whale Project provides the core dataset of ~100,000 entries). CETI now has 50+ researchers across MIT, UC Berkeley, Northwestern, and other institutions. Funded by the Audacious Project (TED). Their approach: record everything, find structure with ML, then work toward meaning.

Earth Species Project (ESP)

A nonprofit focused on bioacoustics AI across many species. Released NatureLM-audio, a foundation model for animal sounds across thousands of species. Their killer whale work with Raincoast Conservation Foundation is expanding in 2026. Co-organized the first AI for Non-Human Animal Communication workshop at NeurIPS. Less focused on translation, more on detection, classification, and monitoring tools.

Dominica Sperm Whale Project

The long-running field operation (Shane Gero) that produces the recordings CETI's ML work depends on. Over two decades of data from one Eastern Caribbean clan of females and young. This is where the 8,719 codas in the 2024 Nature Communications paper came from, and the 3,948 codas in the 2026 Proc. R. Soc. B vowel paper.

Why 5-click codas? 73.3% of all codas in the repertoire are 5-click patterns. With two dominant types (1+1+3 at slow tempo, 5R1 at fast tempo), 5-click codas may serve as the conversational backbone—the "common ground" coda type that both whales share, while non-5-click codas carry more specific or individual information that isn't echoed. The very-slow 5-click pattern (matching 1+1+3, the most common coda type) has the highest echo rate at 74.4%.

74% of all turns consist of a single coda before the other whale responds. Multi-coda runs (2+) make up only 26% of turns. This means whale conversation is predominantly one-coda-per-turn—each "utterance" is a single click pattern, and the other whale immediately responds. Extended monologues are rare.

Whales do not gradually converge in tempo over the course of an exchange (mean convergence is slightly negative at −0.0195). Only 45% of exchanges show any convergence at all. Combined with the tight median tempo ratio of 0.995, this means whales match tempo immediately on the first response, or not at all. There is no "warming up" period.

There is no crescendo, no wind-down. Whale exchanges maintain stable acoustic parameters throughout. The slightly higher echo rate in the middle (48.9% vs ~42%) may reflect longer echo chains once matching is established. These are conversations without the dramatic arc that characterizes many human interactions.

Synthesis: What dialogue patterns tell us. Sperm whale conversation is structurally unlike human dialogue. It is bimodal (echo or contrast, rarely switching), immediate (tempo match happens on the first exchange or not at all), terse (one coda per turn), and flat (no warm-up, no crescendo, no closing ritual). The 5-click coda dominates dialogue the way common words dominate human speech—it is the medium of echo-based matching, the shared vocabulary that both whales can produce and mirror. When whales do break the echo, they modulate tempo, not click count. This two-channel structure (click count for "what type" and tempo for "what variant") functioning differently in dialogue than in repertoire statistics is evidence of pragmatic structure—rules governing how codas are used in context, distinct from the combinatorial rules governing how they are constructed.

Key Papers

Contextual and combinatorial structure in sperm whale vocalisations Nature Comms

Sharma, Gero, Payne, Gruber, Rus, Torralba, Andreas · May 2024

The "phonetic alphabet" paper. Analyzed 8,719 codas from the Eastern Caribbean clan. Found that codas are explained by four independently modulated properties—rhythm, tempo, rubato, and ornamentation—producing 156 distinguishable coda types. Rubato and ornamentation vary with social context; rhythm and tempo do not.

Paper (PDF) · Code (Project-CETI org)

The phonology of sperm whale coda vowels Proc. R. Soc. B

Beguš, Sprouse, Gruber, Gero et al. · April 2026

Analyzed 3,948 codas from 15 whales (females and young, Dominica, 2014–2018). Found two formant-based coda types: "a-codas" (one formant, longer) and "i-codas" (two formants). These pattern like vowels—not just acoustically similar, but structurally organized. I-codas show a bimodal duration distribution (short i vs. long ī).

DOI: 10.1098/rspb.2025.2994 · Open access

WhAM: Towards A Translative Model of Sperm Whale Vocalization NeurIPS 2025

Paradise, Muralikrishnan et al. (UC Berkeley, Project CETI, Northwestern) · Dec 2025

A transformer model that generates synthetic codas from any audio prompt. Finetuned VampNet on 10k recordings. Evaluated with Fréchet Audio Distance and perceptual studies by marine biologists. Its learned representations achieve strong classification performance on rhythm, social unit, and vowel tasks despite being trained for generation.

Open Questions & Future Directions

What do any codas mean? fundamental

Structure has been found (alphabet, vowels), but no coda has been mapped to a meaning. The researchers note that understanding semantic capabilities will require in-depth and interactive research, including playback experiments in natural settings.

Is this language? definitional

Combinatorial structure and phonology are necessary conditions for language but not sufficient. Human language also requires syntax, semantics, displacement (talking about absent things), and productivity (novel sentences). None of these have been demonstrated in whale codas yet.

How do other populations differ? generalization

Almost all ML work uses recordings from one clan off Dominica. Other sperm whale populations likely communicate in different ways. The Mediterranean dialect report (if confirmed) would be the first step toward comparative phonology.

Can WhAM's representations reveal semantics? ML frontier

WhAM was trained for generation but developed useful classification representations as a byproduct. If paired with behavioral data (what were the whales doing when they produced each coda?), these representations might help cluster codas by function.

Data scale infrastructure

Estimates of data needed vary wildly: some experts cite a billion clicks (~100–200 million codas), while WhAM was trained on 10k recordings. The AVATARS robotic framework should accelerate collection, but the gap between current data and what translation might require remains large.

Ethical boundaries ethics

Should researchers play back synthetic codas to wild whales? Playback experiments are seen as a necessary next step for validating meaning, but could also disturb the animals. Commentators have also flagged concerns about tech company funding and potential exploitation of findings.

Build-On-Others Opportunities

For someone entering this field. The most accessible entry points for building on existing work:
Behavioral annotation layer

The biggest gap: codas exist in ML-ready form, but behavioral context (what the whales were doing) is not systematically paired with acoustic data. Building a coda-to-behavior annotation pipeline on top of CETI's data-ingest would be high-impact.

Cross-population comparison

Apply sw-combinatoriality methods to recordings from non-Caribbean whale populations. This tests whether the 156-coda alphabet and the four-feature structure are universal or dialect-specific.

Probe WhAM's latent space

WhAM's learned representations are strong for classification despite generative training. Probing these representations with behavioral labels, social-unit labels, or environmental context could reveal functional clustering that raw acoustics don't show.

CetaVox-style open tooling

CetaVox's workbench idea (segment, catalogue, hypothesize) fills a real gap. CETI's repos are research code, not user-facing tools. A well-built analysis workbench that wraps CETI's models for non-ML researchers would lower the barrier to entry significantly.

Multimodal integration

Combine ESP's NatureLM-audio (cross-species audio understanding) with CETI's sperm-whale-specific models. Could enable transfer learning between species or reveal shared communication structures across cetaceans.
